How to hire a locksmith without getting scammed

From the blog · Oct 23, 2026

Contact us 385-557-7669

Find a locksmith before you need one and save the number. When you call, ask for the business’s name and the full price, including the service call, mileage and parts. When they arrive, expect a marked vehicle and ID, be wary of anyone who insists on drilling the lock, get an itemized invoice, and pay by card. If the price changes on site, the Better Business Bureau says to have someone else do the job.

Most people look for a locksmith at the worst possible moment: standing outside a locked door, late, cold or in a hurry. Scammers count on that. Both the Better Business Bureau (BBB) and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) have warned about locksmiths who advertise as local, quote a low price and then charge far more. Here’s what they say to watch for, and how to protect yourself.

How the scam works

The BBB’s scam alert describes a pattern:

  • Borrowed identity. Many locksmith scammers use the name and logo of a reputable locksmith in their advertising.
  • A generic greeting. When you call, you may hear something like “Hello, locksmith services” instead of a business name.
  • A lowball quote. The caller quotes a very low price, as low as $15 in the BBB’s example.
  • The upsell on site. Once there, the locksmith finds reasons to raise the price. The BBB says victims often pay much more than they were quoted.
  • Drilling and “security” upgrades. Scammers may claim the lock can’t be opened without drilling and replacing it with an expensive lock, sometimes one that is cheap and not actually secure.

There’s a further risk the BBB points out: once a scammer has been to your home or workplace, they know where it is, and may be able to get back in later.

The FTC raised similar concerns as far back as 2008, warning that some locksmiths advertising in local listings may not be local at all, and that others may not have professional training.

Before you’re locked out

The FTC’s first piece of advice is the simplest: do the research before you need a locksmith, the same way you would choose a plumber, electrician or other professional, and then program the number into your phone. A calm afternoon is a much better time to compare businesses than a dark parking lot.

  • Look at the advertising. The BBB suggests checking whether the specific name of the business is clearly identified, and whether the same ad appears under different names.
  • Look the business up on BBB.org.
  • Save the number of the one you choose.

On the phone

The BBB’s advice for the call itself:

  • Ask for the business name. If the person answering won’t give one, that’s a warning sign.
  • Ask about all costs first. That includes the service call, mileage and parts, before you agree to the work.
  • Get an estimate before work begins, including for emergency service.

When the locksmith arrives

  • Check identification. The BBB says most legitimate locksmiths arrive in a clearly marked vehicle and provide identification.
  • Compare the price. If the on-site estimate doesn’t match the phone quote, the BBB says to have someone else do the job.
  • Be wary of drilling. The BBB notes that most locksmiths have the skills to open almost any lock, and that it isn’t routine for a locksmith to insist on drilling the lock to open it.

Paying

  • Insist on an itemized invoice. The BBB says it should list parts, labor, mileage and service charges, plus the business’s name and address.
  • Pay by credit card for added security.

General scam warning signs

The FTC’s general advice on avoiding scams applies here too. One of the most common signs is pressure: scammers usually push you to act quickly so you don’t have time to check what they’re saying. A legitimate locksmith can tell you the price and wait while you decide. If you think you’ve been scammed, you can report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Common questions

01When should I find a locksmith?

Before you need one. The FTC suggests researching a locksmith the same way you would a plumber or electrician, then saving the number in your phone.

02What’s the most common locksmith scam?

The BBB describes a very low quote on the phone, sometimes as low as $15, followed by reasons on site to raise the price, often by hundreds of dollars.

03How should I pay a locksmith?

The BBB recommends paying by credit card for added security and insisting on an itemized invoice listing parts, labor, mileage and service charges.

04What if the price changes when they arrive?

The BBB says if the on-site estimate doesn’t match the phone quote, have someone else do the job.

05How can I tell if a locksmith ad is fake?

The BBB suggests checking whether the specific business name is clearly identified, and whether the same ad appears under other names. Scammers often borrow a reputable locksmith’s name and logo.

06Should a locksmith show ID?

Yes. The BBB says most legitimate locksmiths arrive in a clearly marked vehicle and provide identification.

07Where do I report a locksmith scam?

The FTC takes reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. You can also check and report businesses through the BBB.

08Is there a risk after the job is done?

The BBB warns that once a scammer has been to your home or workplace, they know where it is and may be able to get back in later, which is one more reason to vet a locksmith first.
