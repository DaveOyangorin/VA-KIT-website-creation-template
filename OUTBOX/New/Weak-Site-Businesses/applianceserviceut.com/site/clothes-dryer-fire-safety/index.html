Clothes dryer fire safety: the lint habit that matters most

From the blog · Oct 22, 2026

Schedule Service (801) 487-8591

Most dryer fires are preventable. NFPA says the leading cause of home clothes dryer fires is failure to clean them. Clean the lint filter before or after every load, never run the dryer without it, clean the vent pipe at least once a year, use metal venting, and turn the dryer off when you leave home or go to bed.

The clothes dryer is one of the hardest-working appliances in the house, and one of the easiest to forget about. It just runs. But it also pushes hot air through a tumbling drum full of fabric, and that produces lint, a highly combustible material. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) and the U.S. Fire Administration (USFA) both point to the same simple fact: most dryer fires come down to cleaning that didn’t happen.

How big is the problem?

NFPA research found that from 2010 to 2014, U.S. fire departments responded to an estimated 15,970 home fires a year involving clothes dryers or washing machines, causing an estimated 13 civilian deaths, 440 civilian injuries and $238 million in direct property damage each year. Dust, fiber or lint was the item most often first ignited in fires involving dryers.

A USFA report focused on dryers found an estimated 2,900 clothes dryer fires in residential buildings each year, causing about 5 deaths, 100 injuries and $35 million in property loss annually. Its key finding: failure to clean (34 percent) was the leading factor contributing to these fires.

Why lint is dangerous

USFA explains how it happens. A dryer works by forcing hot air through a turning drum, and a full load of wet clothes can hold as much as a gallon and a half of water. As clothes tumble, they shed small fibers. Much of that lint is caught by the filter, but some travels into the vent with the moist air. Lint is highly combustible, and as it builds up in the dryer and the vent, airflow drops. Vents can also be blocked by bird or animal nests or by crushed or damaged ducting. When the dryer can’t exhaust properly, it can overheat, and a fire can start.

The habits that prevent dryer fires

Every load

  • Clean the lint filter before or after each load (NFPA); USFA says before and after each cycle.
  • Never use the dryer without a lint filter.
  • Remove lint that collects around the drum, and don’t forget the back of the dryer, where USFA notes lint can build up.
  • Don’t overload the dryer, and follow the manufacturer’s operating instructions.
  • Turn the dryer off if you leave home or when you go to bed.

At least once a year

  • Clean the vent pipe. NFPA says to clean lint out of the vent pipe once a year, or more often if clothes are taking longer than normal to dry, or have a dryer lint removal service do it.
  • Check the venting system behind the dryer to make sure it isn’t damaged, crushed or restricted (USFA).
  • Check the outdoor vent flap opens when the dryer is running, and isn’t covered by snow in winter (NFPA).

Installation and setup

  • Have your dryer installed and serviced by a professional, as both NFPA and USFA recommend.
  • Use metal venting. NFPA recommends rigid or flexible metal venting material to keep proper airflow and drying time.
  • Gas dryers should be inspected by a qualified professional to make sure the gas line and connection are intact and free of leaks.
  • Electrical: dryers should be properly grounded, with the right plug and outlet, and connected properly.

USFA’s report also notes that many newer homes put laundry in upstairs bedrooms, hallways or closets, which can mean longer vent runs to reach an outside wall. Long or winding vents collect more lint, so they deserve extra attention.

Around the dryer

  • Keep the area clear of things that can burn, like boxes, cleaning supplies and clothing.
  • Clothes that have touched flammable substances such as gasoline, paint thinner or similar solvents should be laid outside to dry first, then washed and dried as usual.

Warning signs

NFPA’s clearest warning sign is a load that’s taking longer than normal to dry. That usually means air isn’t moving through the vent the way it should, and it’s time to clean the vent pipe. A dryer that has slowed down isn’t just wasting energy; it’s telling you lint has built up somewhere.

Winter matters most

USFA found residential dryer fires happen more often in fall and winter, peaking in January. More laundry, heavier clothes and longer drying times add up, and in Utah, snow can block an outdoor vent flap. A winter check of the vent flap and a vent cleaning before the cold months are cheap insurance.

Common questions

The short version

  • Clean the lint filter every load, and never run the dryer without one.
  • Clean the vent pipe at least once a year, or whenever drying slows down.
  • Use metal venting, and check that the outside flap opens and isn’t snowed in.
  • Turn the dryer off when you leave or go to bed.
