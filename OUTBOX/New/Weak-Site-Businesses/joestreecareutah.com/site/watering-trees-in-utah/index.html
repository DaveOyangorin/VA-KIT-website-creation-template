How to water trees in Utah: deep, slow and less often

From the blog · Oct 22, 2026

Free Quote (801) 289-6689

Water Utah trees slowly and deeply rather than a little every day. USU Extension recommends keeping soil moist 18–20 inches down, irrigating at least weekly in hot, dry summer months, and using a slow hose, soaker hose or drip emitters instead of lawn sprinklers. Check the soil with a long screwdriver before you water, and mulch around the trunk.

In a dry state, trees depend on us. When summer water restrictions hit or the sprinkler schedule gets trimmed, trees are often the first thing to suffer and the last thing anyone notices. USU Extension puts the stakes plainly: a brown lawn comes back easily when water returns, but an under-watered tree may die, and it can take decades to replace. Here’s what Utah State University’s extension specialists recommend.

Trees come first

USU Extension calls trees undoubtedly the most valuable plants in a landscape, and says they must be prioritized for irrigation during drought. Grass easily recovers from a long dry spell, so it belongs last on the priority list. If you have to choose where water goes this summer, choose the trees.

Water deep, not often

Tree roots grow deeper than lawn roots, so they need a different approach. USU’s guidance:

  • Go deep. Slow, deep irrigation should reach at least 10 to 20 inches of soil. USU’s drought tips recommend keeping soil moist to 18–20 inches for trees and shrubs.
  • Skip the daily sprinkle. Short, frequent watering only wets the top few inches. USU warns this promotes shallow roots, leaving trees poorly anchored and vulnerable to wind and drought.
  • Water wide. A tree’s roots can extend out close to its height when water is available, so USU suggests watering an area about two-thirds of that distance from the trunk.

How often?

USU’s irrigation fact sheet says that in Utah’s dry summer months, it’s essential to irrigate trees at least once a week. Soil type changes the picture: clay soil absorbs water slowly but holds more of it, so trees in clay can go longer between waterings, while trees in sandy or sandy loam soil need more frequent irrigation. Spring and fall need less water, because less is lost from plants and soil.

USU’s drought guidance adds a water-saving strategy: withhold water until just before stress sets in, which may be roughly every few weeks depending on conditions, and learn your particular tree’s needs.

Check the soil first

You don’t need special equipment. USU suggests a long screwdriver or metal rod as a moisture probe: it slides easily into moist soil and stops when it hits dry soil. You can also use the “feel method”: take a handful of soil from about 4–8 inches down and squeeze it. Sandy and loam soils are dry when they run through your fingers without leaving a stain; clay is hard to break apart when dry. At the right moisture, the soil forms a ball and leaves a thin stain on your hand, with no water running out.

Use the right tools

USU is specific: overhead sprinklers are ideal for small plants and shrubs, but not for trees. Sprinklers put out water faster than soil can absorb it, so it runs off before reaching deep soil. Instead, USU recommends:

  • A slow-running hose or shower hose placed at the root zone.
  • A soaker hose or a sprinkler turned on low over the roots during the coolest part of the day.
  • Drip emitters, with flow rates of 1 to 8 gallons per hour suggested for trees.

Soil type sets the pace. USU notes sandy soils absorb water at about 2 inches per hour, loam at about three-quarters of an inch, and clay at about half an inch per hour. If water starts running off, irrigate in cycles instead of all at once.

How much water?

USU’s numbers give a sense of scale:

  • Shrubs and smaller trees (up to a 4-inch trunk diameter) may need about half an inch to 1 inch of water weekly from sprinklers, or roughly 5 to 50 gallons with drip, depending on size.
  • Large trees (over a 4-inch trunk) may need hundreds of gallons of water per week.
  • Amounts run higher in southern Utah and lower in northern Utah.

For a precise number, USU Extension offers a free tree irrigation calculator that uses your tree’s canopy width, local evapotranspiration data from the Utah Climate Center, and your soil type.

Signs of too little, and too much

Wilting and leaf scorch are classic signs of water stress. But USU warns that over-watering can cause similar browning, and even leaf drop, because waterlogged soil starves roots of oxygen. Many gardeners mistake those symptoms for thirst and add more water, making it worse. Check the soil before reacting to the leaves.

New trees need extra care

USU’s post-planting advice: water thoroughly at planting, then about once a week through the first growing season (adjusted for significant rain), more often on sandy soils or in very hot weather. A light sprinkle isn’t enough; water has to reach 6 to 12 inches down to encourage deep roots.

Mulch helps

USU calls mulching one of the best ways to keep trees healthy: a circle of wood chips, compost or other coarse organic mulch at least 4 feet across and about 4 inches deep. It controls weeds, keeps roots moist, reduces soil compaction and keeps mowers away from the trunk. Controlling grass and weeds around the trunk also reduces competition for water.

Common questions

01How often should I water an established tree in summer?

USU Extension recommends irrigating trees at least once a week in Utah’s dry summer months, with slow, deep watering. Clay soils hold water longer; sandy soils need more frequent watering.

02How deep should I water a tree?

USU Extension says to keep soil moist to a depth of 18–20 inches for trees and shrubs, and that slow, deep irrigation should reach at least 10 to 20 inches of soil.

03Are lawn sprinklers enough for trees?

Usually not. USU explains that sprinklers put out water faster than soil can absorb it, so it runs off before reaching deep roots. A slow-running hose, soaker hose or drip emitters work better.

04How much should I water a newly planted tree?

USU Extension says to water thoroughly at planting and about once a week through the first growing season, more often on sandy soils or in very hot weather, so water reaches 6 to 12 inches down.

The short version

  • Trees first, lawn last, when water is tight.
  • Water slowly and deeply, 18–20 inches down, at least weekly in summer.
  • Use a hose, soaker or drip, not lawn sprinklers.
  • Probe the soil with a screwdriver before you water.
  • Mulch a wide ring around the trunk.
