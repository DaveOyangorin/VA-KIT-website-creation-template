How high should you mow a Utah lawn?

From the blog · Oct 22, 2026

Get a quote 801-244-4035

Mow most Utah lawns at about 3 inches. USU Extension says common lawn grasses can be cut anywhere from 2 to 4 inches, but 3 inches improves heat and drought tolerance. Never take off more than a third of the blade at once, mow shady areas a little higher, and leave the clippings on the lawn.

Mowing is the job everyone does and almost nobody thinks about. But how high you cut changes how deep the roots grow, how much water the lawn needs and how many weeds move in. Utah State University Extension has published clear numbers for Utah lawns. Here they are.

The number: about 3 inches

In its fact sheet Basic Turfgrass Care, USU Extension says the common lawn species in Utah can all be mowed at a height of 2 to 4 inches, and recommends mowing at 3 inches to improve heat and drought tolerance.

The reason is simple: in USU’s words, taller grass means deeper roots. Deeper roots reach more of the water in the soil, which matters in a dry Utah summer.

USU’s newer fact sheet on drought resilience (April 2026) makes the same point from another angle: a higher mowing height helps shade the soil surface, reduce evaporation and support deeper rooting.

Why not just cut it short?

A short cut looks tidy for a day, but it works against the lawn. Cutting low removes the leaf area grass uses to feed itself, exposes more soil to the sun (USU’s 2026 fact sheet notes a higher cut shades the soil and reduces evaporation), and, by USU’s logic that taller grass means deeper roots, leaves you with a shallower-rooted lawn that needs water more often.

The rule: never cut more than one third

USU Extension’s rule is to never mow more than one third of the total length of the grass blades off at a time. If you want a 3-inch lawn, mow before the grass passes about 4½ inches.

Fell behind after a vacation or a wet spring? Don’t scalp it all at once. USU advises lowering the height gradually over several mowings.

Shade: mow a little higher

Grass in the shade needs more leaf to collect the light it gets. USU Extension recommends mowing shaded areas higher than sunny ones. If part of your yard sits under mature trees or on the north side of the house, set the deck a notch up there.

Leave the clippings

Bagging clippings is extra work you can usually skip. USU Extension says to return or mulch the clippings whenever possible, because they return nitrogen to the soil and help decrease evaporation. The 2026 drought fact sheet lists returning clippings as one of the ways to build healthier soil and deeper roots.

Mowing as weed control

It sounds too easy, but USU Extension states that proper mowing controls most weeds.

When to mow in Utah

USU Extension lists the mowing season as March to November in the northern region and February to November in the southern region. Growth speeds up and slows down with the weather, so mow by the height of the grass, not the calendar: when it’s about a third taller than your target, it’s time.

Mowing works with watering

Height and water go together. USU’s turfgrass guidance recommends watering in the early morning when it’s cool and calm, avoiding 10 AM to 6 PM, and applying between ½ and 1 inch of water per irrigation. A lawn mowed at 3 inches with deep roots makes the most of every one of those waterings. And if drought pushes the lawn dormant and golden, USU notes it recovers when water returns; just keep heavy traffic off it in the meantime.

Fertilizing and aeration fit around mowing

A lawn mowed at the right height still needs feeding. USU Extension says cool-season grasses need 2 to 5 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet a year, but typical home lawns under moderate maintenance need only 2 to 3 pounds; the higher end is for intensively used turf. Timing follows the grass’s growth pattern, and USU lists April, June and October for both regions.

Aeration helps water soak in and reduces compaction and thatch. USU’s windows for the northern region are March to May and September to October. The April 2026 drought fact sheet also lists aeration to relieve compaction, along with appropriate fertilization, among the practices that support deeper rooting.

Put together, that’s a simple rhythm for a northern Utah lawn: mow at about 3 inches from March through November, feed in April, June and October, aerate in spring or early fall, and water deeply in the cool early morning.

Quick checklist

  • Set the mower to about 3 inches (anywhere from 2 to 4 works for common Utah grasses).
  • Never remove more than a third of the blade in one cut.
  • Lower an overgrown lawn gradually over several mowings.
  • Mow shaded areas a little higher.
  • Leave or mulch the clippings.
  • Mow by height, from about March to November in northern Utah.
  • Fertilize in April, June and October; typical home lawns need only 2–3 lbs of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft a year.
