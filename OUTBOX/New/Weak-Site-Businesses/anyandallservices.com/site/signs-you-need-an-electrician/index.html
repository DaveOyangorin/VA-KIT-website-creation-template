6 signs you need an electrician, according to NFPA

From the blog · Oct 22, 2026

Request an estimate 801-717-9125

Call a qualified electrician if you notice any of the six warning signs on NFPA’s electrical safety tip sheet: breakers that trip or fuses that blow often, a tingle when you touch an appliance, discolored or warm outlets, a burning or rubbery smell from an appliance, flickering or dimming lights, or sparks from an outlet. UL adds buzzing outlets and kitchens or bathrooms without GFCI outlets to the list.

Most electrical problems give some warning before they become dangerous. The trouble is that the warnings are easy to live with: a light that flickers when the dryer starts, an outlet that’s always a little warm, a breaker you reset every week. Here’s what the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) and UL say those signs mean, and what to do about them.

NFPA’s six warning signs

NFPA’s electrical safety tip sheet, used by fire departments across the country, ends with an “Important Reminder.” Call a qualified electrician (or your landlord) if you have:

  1. Frequent problems with blowing fuses or tripping circuit breakers.
  2. A tingling feeling when you touch an electrical appliance.
  3. Discolored or warm wall outlets.
  4. A burning or rubbery smell coming from an appliance.
  5. Flickering or dimming lights.
  6. Sparks from an outlet.

NFPA’s broader advice is simple: have all electrical work done by a qualified electrician.

What each sign usually means

UL Solutions, the safety science company, published its own list of signs of a wiring problem. It adds useful detail on several of NFPA’s:

Breakers that trip, fuses that blow

UL’s rule of thumb: if one appliance trips a breaker wherever you plug it in, the appliance is likely the problem. If one outlet keeps blowing the same fuse, the circuit is probably overloaded, and it’s worth asking a professional about upgrading the circuit or adding a new line.

Flickering or dimming lights

UL notes the light fixture is rarely the culprit. More often, energy-hungry appliances such as heaters or washing machines share the circuit. An electrician can move lights to other circuits or add dedicated lines for major appliances.

Warm or hot outlets

A switch plate may feel slightly warm, but UL says an uncomfortably hot outlet is not normal. Turn off whatever is plugged in and try another outlet. If an outlet gets hot with nothing plugged into it, UL says it may be wired incorrectly, and an electrician may advise turning off its breaker until the system is serviced.

Strange smells

A brand-new appliance may smell briefly the first time it runs. But UL says an odd smell from an outlet means you should unplug what’s connected and stop using it until a qualified electrician inspects it, and a strange odor from a breaker panel or fuse box warrants calling an electrician right away.

Sparks

UL’s verdict: sparking is never a good sign. Sparks from a breaker panel, fuse box or outlet need an electrician as soon as possible. If an appliance sparks, the appliance may be the problem, and an appliance repair technician can test it.

Two more signs from UL

  • Buzzing. Properly working electricity is silent. UL says loose prongs, loose outlets or frayed wiring can cause buzzing; if it’s coming from an outlet, stop using it and call an electrician.
  • Standard outlets in kitchens or bathrooms. Because water conducts electricity, UL says these rooms need ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) outlets, the kind with two small buttons in the center. If yours look like standard outlets, have them replaced with GFCIs.

Everyday habits that prevent problems

NFPA’s tip sheet also lists habits that keep a home’s wiring out of trouble:

  • One heat-producing appliance per outlet. Plug only one coffee maker, toaster, space heater or similar appliance into a receptacle outlet at a time.
  • Major appliances go straight into the wall. Refrigerators, dryers, washers, stoves, air conditioners and microwaves should be plugged directly into a wall outlet, not extension cords or plug strips.
  • Extension cords are temporary. If you rely on them, have a qualified electrician add more outlets. UL agrees: use extension cords sparingly and for short periods.
  • Keep cords out of the way. Don’t run cords across doorways or under carpets, and don’t use frayed or damaged cords.
  • Use the right bulb. Check the fixture’s sticker for the correct wattage.
  • Test AFCIs and GFCIs once a month, following the manufacturer’s recommendations.
  • Get an inspection when buying or remodeling. NFPA recommends having the home inspected by a qualified inspector or according to local requirements.

UL also suggests checking your breaker panel or fuse box for the date of the last inspection, noting that the Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends having a professional check your system every 10 years.

Why it matters

NFPA points out that you don’t need a flame to start a fire: fires can start when heat builds up near things that burn, such as a hot bulb near cloth or a cord under a carpet. UL cites the Electrical Safety Foundation International’s estimate that electrical malfunctions cause more than 50,000 house fires a year, and notes that most can be prevented.

Common questions

01When should I call an electrician?

NFPA says to call a qualified electrician if you have frequent problems with blowing fuses or tripping breakers, a tingling feeling when you touch an appliance, discolored or warm outlets, a burning or rubbery smell from an appliance, flickering or dimming lights, or sparks from an outlet.

02Are flickering lights dangerous?

They’re one of NFPA’s warning signs. UL notes the fixture itself is rarely the cause; energy-hungry appliances on the same circuit are more likely, and an electrician can move lights to another circuit or add dedicated lines.

03Is it normal for an outlet to feel warm?

A switch plate may feel slightly warm, but UL says an uncomfortably hot outlet is not normal. If an outlet gets hot with nothing plugged in, it may be wired incorrectly.

04Can I use extension cords long term?

No. NFPA says extension cords are intended for temporary use; have a qualified electrician add more outlets instead.
