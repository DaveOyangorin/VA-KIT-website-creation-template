Why birds hit your windows, and how to stop it

From the blog · Oct 21, 2026

Instant Quote 801-518-4062

Birds hit windows because they can’t tell glass from the sky, trees or open space they see in it. Almost half of all bird collisions happen at homes. The fix is to give birds a visible pattern on the outside of the glass, with gaps no wider than 2 inches, starting with your biggest and most reflective windows and any near a feeder.

That thud on the living room window is a familiar sound in a lot of Utah homes, especially in spring and fall. Sometimes the bird flies off. Often it doesn’t. It’s easy to assume window strikes are rare accidents or a problem for glass skyscrapers downtown, but bird conservation groups say the opposite: ordinary houses account for a huge share of collisions, and most of them are preventable with simple, inexpensive changes. Here’s what the research says, which windows matter most, and what works.

How big is the problem?

American Bird Conservancy (ABC) estimates that more than 1 billion birds die from collisions with glass every year in the U.S. Audubon cites a 2014 study by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the Smithsonian Institution that put the number killed by building collisions at between 365 million and one billion a year, which makes glass one of the leading direct human causes of bird deaths.

And the numbers we see undercount it. Audubon notes that for every collision victim found, about three more typically go unseen: they fly out of sight before falling, or are carried off by predators.

It’s mostly a home-window problem

People tend to picture lit-up towers at night. ABC’s research points somewhere else entirely: most collisions happen during the day, and almost half happen at home windows. Low-rise buildings account for nearly all of the rest. Buildings over 11 stories cause only about 0.1 percent of building collisions in the U.S., simply because there are so few of them.

In other words, the windows that matter most for birds are the ones on houses like yours.

Why birds can’t see glass

People don’t really see glass either. We bump into clean, unmarked doors too. The difference is that we learn, from a young age, the cues that say “glass is here”: window frames, door handles, mullions, where we are in a building. ABC explains that birds don’t learn those cues, so glass is effectively invisible to them. A bird may learn about one particular window near where it lives, if it survives hitting it, but it can’t generalize that lesson to other glass.

ABC describes two main reasons birds hit windows:

  • Reflections. Glass reflects sky, trees and landscape, and birds treat the reflection as real space they can fly into.
  • See-through glass. Birds try to reach habitat, open sky or plants they can see through the glass, such as a window on the far side of a room, a glass corner, or a glass railing.

Because birds collide at speed, and their small bodies are built on hollow bones, the impact is often fatal or causes injuries that kill them later.

When strikes happen

Collisions happen year-round, but ABC says they’re most frequent during spring and fall migration, with the largest number in the fall, when young birds are migrating alongside adults. Resident birds, especially juveniles, collide more in summer as they explore. Audubon agrees that the most important times to take action are spring and fall migration and the local breeding season.

Light plays a part too. ABC notes that artificial light at night draws night-migrating birds into developed areas, where they rest and then forage the next morning, among windows. Turning off unneeded outdoor and indoor lights overnight, or at least before birds come down in the early morning, reduces the number of birds around buildings.

Which windows to fix first

You don’t have to treat every window in the house. ABC says the majority of collisions are often caused by a small number of problem windows, and recommends starting with:

  1. Windows that have already had strikes.
  2. Large windows and glass doors, especially ones that reflect sky or greenery, or that you can see sky or plants through.
  3. Windows across from bird feeders, bird baths and fruit-bearing plants.

In many cases, ABC says, fixing only these windows will stop almost all of your collisions.

What works: the 2-inch rule

ABC’s research shows most collisions can be prevented with patterns that are the right size and spacing, applied to the outside of the glass:

  • Spacing: follow the two-inch-by-two-inch rule. Gaps between marks, vertical, horizontal or diagonal, should be 2 inches or less. Small songbirds, including hummingbirds, can fly through 4-inch gaps. (Audubon’s guidance is similar: multiple markings 2 to 4 inches apart.)
  • Size: stripes at least one-eighth of an inch wide; dots at least one-quarter inch across. Bigger is better.
  • Visibility: you should be able to see the pattern clearly from 10 feet away, so birds have time to change course.
  • Color: white tends to work best, because it reflects the most light and contrasts with most backgrounds and reflections.

Any pattern that follows those rules works. It doesn’t have to be dots or stripes.

Options, from free to finished

  • Exterior insect screens. ABC lists full external screens among the most effective fixes, and recommends them even on windows that don’t open.
  • Tempera paint. A DIY option: paint a pattern on the outside of the glass that follows the 2-inch rule.
  • Tape and decals. Translucent bird tape or adhesive dots applied in strips on the outside of the glass.
  • Hanging cords. Vertical cords hung in front of a window, close enough together to follow the spacing rule.
  • Films. Patterned films, or solid films that cover the whole window.

Audubon also suggests closing blinds or curtains, moving indoor plants away from windows, and putting feeders directly on windows rather than a short flight away.

Inside or outside?

Outside. ABC is clear that products applied to the inside of the glass should be avoided whenever possible, because strong reflections on the outer surface hide them from birds. For a window you truly can’t reach from outside, ABC suggests a test: put something large and bright, like sticky notes, on the inside, then look at the window from outdoors every two or three hours starting early in the morning. If you can clearly see your test pattern through the reflections, birds might too.

New windows can make it worse

People often notice more strikes after replacing windows. ABC lists three common reasons:

  • More reflection. Energy-efficient coatings and extra panes can make new windows more reflective than old ones.
  • Less visible screens. Screens cut reflections, but on newer windows they’re often between panes of glass or cover only the bottom half.
  • Bigger glass. New windows often lack the muntins and mullions, the dividers, that once gave birds a visual cue.

If you’re replacing windows, ABC suggests full external screens, applying one of the treatments above, or special-ordering a bird-friendly glass from its solutions database.

Getting help with hard-to-reach glass

Most of these fixes are quick DIY jobs on ground-floor windows. For second-story or awkward glass, ABC suggests enlisting a local repairperson, house painter or window washer: putting a treatment in place will be a quick job for them.

Common questions

01Why do birds fly into my windows?

Birds can’t read the cues people use to recognize glass. They see reflections of sky, trees or landscape as real, or they try to fly through clear glass to habitat or open space they can see on the other side.

02What actually stops window strikes?

Markings on the outside of the glass, spaced no more than 2 inches apart, that are visible from about 10 feet away. External insect screens and solid films that cover the whole window also work.

03Do decals on the inside of the window work?

Usually not well. American Bird Conservancy says products applied on the inside can be invisible to birds when reflections on the outside are strong. Put them outside whenever you can.

04Which windows should I treat first?

Windows that have already had strikes, large windows and glass doors that reflect sky or greenery, and windows facing bird feeders, bird baths or fruiting plants. Treating those alone often stops almost all collisions.

The short version

  • Almost half of bird-window collisions happen at homes, mostly in daylight.
  • Treat your worst windows first: past strikes, big reflective glass, and glass near feeders.
  • Mark the outside of the glass with a pattern that leaves gaps of 2 inches or less, visible from 10 feet.
  • External screens work. Inside decals usually don’t.
  • Turn off unneeded lights at night, especially during spring and fall migration.
