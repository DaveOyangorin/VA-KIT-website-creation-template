Why topping hurts trees (and what arborists do instead)

From the blog · Oct 22, 2026

Free estimate 315-863-6747

Topping, cutting a tree’s branches back to stubs, is one of the most harmful pruning practices there is, according to the International Society of Arboriculture. It can strip 50 to 100 percent of the leaf-bearing crown, invites decay and sunburn, and the fast regrowth is weakly attached and breaks easily. It doesn’t make a tree safer; ISA says it increases risk in the long run. Proper branch reduction or, sometimes, replacement is the better answer.

In older Salt Lake City neighborhoods, big shade trees have grown up alongside the houses, and sooner or later someone asks for the tree to be “cut back hard.” It’s an understandable request. But the practice it usually leads to, topping, does far more harm than good. Here’s what the International Society of Arboriculture (ISA), the organization that certifies arborists, says about it.

What topping is

ISA defines topping as the indiscriminate cutting of tree branches to stubs or to lateral branches that are not large enough to assume the terminal role. You may also hear it called “heading,” “tipping,” “hat-racking” or “rounding over.”

People usually top a tree to make it smaller, because it feels too big for the property or because tall trees seem risky. ISA’s position is direct: topping is not a viable method of height reduction, it certainly does not reduce future risk, and it will increase risk in the long term. ISA calls it perhaps the most harmful tree pruning practice known.

1. It starves the tree

Leaves are a tree’s food factories. ISA says topping can remove 50 to 100 percent of a tree’s leaf-bearing crown, which can temporarily starve the tree and trigger survival mechanisms. Dormant buds activate and force rapid growth of multiple shoots below each cut. If the tree doesn’t have the stored energy to put out a new crop of leaves, it will be seriously weakened and may die.

A stressed tree with large, open wounds is also more vulnerable to insects and disease. ISA notes it may lack the energy to chemically defend its wounds, and some insects are actually attracted to the chemical signals stressed trees release.

2. It opens the door to decay

Correct pruning cuts are made just beyond the branch collar, where a healthy tree is biologically equipped to close the wound. Topping cuts, made along a limb between laterals, leave stubs with wounds the tree may not be able to close. ISA explains that trees normally “wall off,” or compartmentalize, decaying tissue, but few trees can defend the many severe wounds topping causes, giving decay a free path down through the branches.

3. It can sunburn the bark

A tree’s crown shades its own branches and trunk. Remove the leaves and the remaining bark is suddenly exposed to high light and heat. ISA says the result may be sunburn of the tissue beneath the bark, leading to cankers, bark splitting and the death of some branches.

4. It makes the tree more dangerous

This is the part that surprises most people. The shoots that sprout below a topping cut grow from buds near the surface of the old branch. Unlike normal branches, which develop in a socket of overlapping wood, ISA says these shoots are anchored only in the outermost layers of the parent branch and are weakly attached. They grow fast, up to 20 feet in a year in some species, and they are prone to breaking, especially in wind or ice. The original goal was to reduce risk; the result is more risk of limb failure.

ISA’s own tree risk checklist reflects this: one of its questions is simply, “Has the tree been topped or heavily pruned?”

5. It’s ugly, and it’s expensive

ISA notes that a topped tree loses its natural form for good, looking disfigured when bare and like a dense ball of foliage when in leaf. And the cost doesn’t end with the job:

  • More maintenance. A surviving tree will likely need corrective pruning within a few years; if it dies, it has to be removed.
  • Lower property value. ISA says healthy, well-maintained trees can add 10 to 20 percent to a property’s value, while disfigured, topped trees are considered an impending expense.
  • More liability. Because topping is considered an unacceptable pruning practice, ISA notes that damage from a topped tree’s branch failure may lead to a finding of negligence in court.

What arborists do instead

Sometimes a tree really does need to be smaller, for utility line clearance, for example. ISA describes the right way:

  1. Remove small branches back to their point of origin.
  2. Shorten larger limbs back to a lateral branch that is large enough to take over, at least one third the diameter of the limb being removed. This branch reduction helps preserve the tree’s natural form.
  3. Know when to stop. If very large cuts would be needed, the tree may not be able to close and compartmentalize the wounds. Sometimes the best solution is to remove the tree and replace it with a species better suited to the site.

For trees with weak branches or stems, ISA’s risk guidance adds another option: cabling and bracing, which provides physical support to increase strength and stability, though such supports are not guarantees against failure.

Before you hire anyone to cut

  • Ask how they plan to reduce the tree’s size. If the answer is cutting all the branches back to the same height, that’s topping.
  • Ask whether the work will be done or overseen by an ISA Certified Arborist. ISA says certified arborists have proven their knowledge through experience and a comprehensive exam, and must keep up continuing education.
  • Ask about alternatives: branch reduction, cabling and bracing, or replacement with a better-sized tree.

Common questions

01What is tree topping?

ISA defines topping as the indiscriminate cutting of tree branches to stubs or to lateral branches that are not large enough to assume the terminal role. It also goes by heading, tipping, hat-racking and rounding over.

02Does topping make a tree safer?

No. ISA says topping is not a viable method of height reduction, does not reduce future risk, and will increase risk in the long term, because the regrowth is weakly attached and prone to breaking.

03How much of the tree does topping remove?

ISA says topping can remove 50 to 100 percent of a tree’s leaf-bearing crown, which can temporarily starve the tree.

04What should be done instead?

Remove small branches back to their point of origin, and shorten larger limbs back to a lateral branch at least one third the diameter of the limb being removed. Sometimes the best answer is removing the tree and planting a better-suited species.
