What Temperature Should Your Water Heater Be? 120, 140, and the Trade-Off

By St. George Home Services · October 20, 2026

(435) 850-0053Ask for a visit

The short answer most homes get is 120°F. That is the setting the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission urges, because scalds are the injury that actually happens in houses. The longer answer is that a tank kept cooler, with scale and sediment in the bottom, is better at growing bacteria. Here is what each number protects against, and how to pick.

The dial on a water heater is one of the few settings in a house that trades one risk against another. Turn it up, and the water at the tap can burn. Turn it down too far, and the tank sits at a temperature some bacteria like. St. George adds a third factor: very hard water, which fills tanks with scale faster than most places.

What 120°F protects against: scalds

The CPSC’s guidance on tap water scalds is blunt about the numbers. Most adults will suffer third-degree burns from water at:

  • 150°F in about two seconds,
  • 140°F in about six seconds,
  • 130°F in about thirty seconds,
  • and even 120°F, with a five-minute exposure.

The CPSC notes that most tap water scald injuries and deaths happen to older adults and to children under five: the people least able to pull away quickly. That is why its recommendation is to set residential water heaters to 120°F, and to use the lowest setting that still meets your household’s needs. The same agency says to always hand-test the water before bathing a baby or a young child, whatever the setting.

What 140°F protects against: bacteria in the tank

The other side of the trade is Legionella, the bacterium behind Legionnaires’ disease. OSHA’s guidance on Legionella control gives its ideal growth range as 68 to 122°F, and warns that water heaters kept below 140°F that contain scale and sediment may foster its growth. Stagnant water makes it worse: lines that are rarely used, or plumbing that was capped off and left in place.

OSHA lists storing hot water at a minimum of 140°F, and delivering it at no less than 122°F to every outlet, among the controls for domestic hot water systems. The World Health Organization says much the same: keep hot water above 50°C (122°F), which means water leaving the heater at 60°C (140°F) or above, particularly in hospitals, care settings and aged-care facilities. Both are written mainly with larger and shared buildings in mind, but the biology inside a home tank is the same.

The St. George factor: scale

Here is where local water matters. The City of St. George tells residents to set softeners to 18 grains per gallon, with a maximum of 22, which the U.S. Geological Survey scale classes as very hard. We cover what that does to tanks and tankless units in what St. George water does to a water heater. The short version: hard water leaves scale and sediment in the bottom of a tank, and OSHA names scale and sediment as part of what lets bacteria grow in a cooler tank.

So in this valley, the temperature setting and the maintenance belong in the same conversation. A tank that gets flushed on schedule, with softened or treated water feeding it, is in a very different state from one that has never been drained.

How to choose a setting for your house

There is no single right number for every home. A few honest questions get you most of the way:

  • Who lives here? Young children, or an older adult who might not react quickly in a shower, push the case for 120°F at the tap.
  • Is anyone at higher risk from infection? If someone in the house has a weakened immune system or chronic lung disease, ask your doctor about Legionella risk; it may tip the balance toward storing hotter.
  • How much do you use? A tank in a part-time house, or one serving a bathroom nobody uses, sits stagnant. Running unused taps regularly helps, and a recirculation pump changes how water moves through the lines.
  • When was the tank last drained? Scale and sediment matter on either side of this trade.

Some households get both: they store water hotter and fit a tempering (mixing) valve, so water at the taps comes out cooler. Whether that suits your setup depends on the heater, the plumbing and who lives there, so it is worth asking whoever services the heater.

How to check and adjust it

  1. Measure first. The CPSC’s method is the simplest: hold a candy or meat thermometer under the hot tap first thing in the morning, or at least two hours after anyone has used hot water. The dial markings on many heaters are not degrees.
  2. Electric tanks: power off. Turn off the breaker before opening the access panels. Most electric tanks have two thermostats, upper and lower, and both should be set to the same temperature.
  3. Gas tanks: follow the manual. Thermostats differ between makers; adjust as the manufacturer’s instructions say, then measure again after a few hours.
  4. Tankless units usually set an output temperature on a control panel. Hard water matters even more for these: see the guide above on manufacturer hardness limits, and tankless descaling.

If turning the temperature up makes the tank’s pressure relief valve drip, or you hear knocking after a change, don’t keep adjusting: that can be a sign of thermal expansion or a failing valve, and it should be looked at.

Questions

01What temperature does the CPSC recommend for a water heater?

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission urges lowering residential water heaters to 120°F to reduce the risk of tap water scalds (CPSC Publication 5098).

02Is 120°F hot enough to prevent Legionella?

It is inside the range OSHA describes as ideal for Legionella growth, 68 to 122°F. OSHA and the WHO describe storing hot water at 140°F (60°C) as a control, mainly for larger and shared buildings. For a household with someone at higher risk, it is worth a conversation about storing hotter and limiting the temperature at the tap.

03How do I check my water temperature?

CPSC’s method: hold a candy or meat thermometer under the hot tap first thing in the morning, or at least two hours after anyone has used hot water, and adjust from there.
