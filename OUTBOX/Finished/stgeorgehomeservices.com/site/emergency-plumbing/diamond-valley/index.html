Emergency plumbing

The closest community we cover, and it is not on the city sewer.

Diamond Valley sits just up SR-18 from town, close enough that plenty of advice written for St. George gets applied to it. Most of that advice assumes a city sewer main at the street. Out here it is septic, and that changes both the diagnosis and the things you should refuse to let anybody do.

Right now

Three things, in this order.

  1. Shut the water off at the house. Garage wall, water heater closet, or where the service enters. Do not go hunting for a municipal curb stop — this is not a city system.
  2. If it is backing up, stop using water entirely. Not the washer, not the dishwasher, not another flush “to see.” On septic, every gallon you add while it is backing up goes somewhere you do not want it, and a couple of loads of laundry is the difference between a clog and a floor.
  3. Call us. A person answers at any hour, and will tell you honestly whether it can wait until morning. From here that is usually a real option and the daytime rate is lower.

Diamond Valley, specifically

On septic, the drain is a symptom and the tank is the question.

The water district publishes septic density studies for Diamond Valley — the tool it uses to limit how many systems sit in one area — and does not list it among the wastewater systems it operates. The reading is the obvious one: individual septic, house by house, rather than a municipal main at the street.

That matters because the same symptom means something different. In town, several fixtures slow at once usually means a blockage in the lateral between the house and the city main. Out here the same symptom is just as likely to mean the tank is full, or the drainfield has stopped accepting water. Those are not plumbing repairs. They are a pump-out, or in the bad case a drainfield problem, and no amount of work inside the house fixes either.

So here is the thing to refuse. If somebody turns up and wants to hydro jet your line before anyone has looked at the tank, say no. Jetting a line that drains into a failed field pushes the problem thirty feet further away and buys you a few weeks. It is the most expensive way to not fix something. Camera first, tank level second, and then a decision.

The rest of it is ordinary. Diamond Valley sits at about 4,500 feet, roughly 1,700 feet above St. George, and it is unincorporated — county government, not a city hall, so permits and inspections go through the county rather than a municipal building department.

We do not know from a map whether your particular parcel is on a well, a shared system or something else. Nobody does without looking it up. Tell us the address and we will work it out with you before anyone drives anywhere.
