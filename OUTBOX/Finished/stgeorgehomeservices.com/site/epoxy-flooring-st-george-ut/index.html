Garage floors

What you are actually buying

Not a product. Nearly all of what decides whether a coated floor is still good in five years happens before any color goes down, and none of it is visible afterwards: how much of the slab came off, what the moisture read, and whether the cracks were repaired or painted over.

So we quote the preparation in detail and in writing, rather than quoting a finish and treating the grinding as something that happens on the day. If a quote you are comparing ours against does not mention a surface profile or a moisture test, those are not two prices for the same job.

One number, one quote, and it does not change after the grinder comes out.

What you get in writing

  • The moisture reading, and which published test produced it.
  • The surface profile the finish requires, as a number on the published scale.
  • What happens to each crack, priced as its own line rather than absorbed.
  • If we think your slab should not be coated, you hear that before you have paid for anything.

You keep all of it whether or not you hire us. Plain Terms covers who does the work, the license and insurance behind it, and the one number you call when something is wrong.

Outside the house

What is different about doing this in Washington County

The heat is a working condition, not a talking point

St. George recorded 98 days at or above 100°F in 2024, the most on record, and the all-time high is 117°F. July has now set the record two years running.

That is what the finished floor lives with. It is also a constraint on the work: resin systems have a published application temperature window and their working time shortens as the slab warms. A July install in an uncooled garage is a scheduling problem before it is anything else.

The sand is why the date moves

The Utah Geological Survey maps wind-blown sand as one of nine adverse construction conditions for the St. George and Hurricane area, and Washington County publishes it as its own hazard layer. Most counties do not map blowing sand at all. Utah’s Department of Environmental Quality notes that from spring through fall, high winds and dry ground combine into dust storms that “can lead to extremely high levels of particulate matter.”

Wet resin holds whatever lands in it, permanently. April is the windiest month here. So an exterior date is a forecast-dependent date, and anybody who books you one three weeks out without saying so is promising something they do not control.

The monsoon, and the calendar

Flooding is, in Special Study 127’s words, “the most widespread and damaging geologic hazard in southwestern Utah.” It arrives as short heavy cells after long dry spells — St. George has recorded a 78-day dry spell ending with monsoon onset. An exterior coating cannot go onto a wet slab and cannot cure through a storm.

At the other end of the year, the reliably non-freezing window here runs March 1 to November 21, and resin has a published minimum cure temperature. An exterior slab in January can sit under it.

The ground, and what it does to concrete

Gypsiferous soil and rock is another of those nine mapped conditions, and where sulfate is present in the ground it attacks concrete itself. Under the American Concrete Institute exposure classes, a soil sulfate test determines the mix a slab needs — class S1 at 0.10 to 0.20 per cent water-soluble sulfate, S2 at 0.20 to 2.00 per cent where sulfate-resistant cement is required, S3 above that. St. George culinary water itself runs to 451 parts per million sulfate, inside class S1 for water contact.

We do not test soil and we are not going to tell you your slab is failing. We will tell you whether your address sits in a mapped zone, show you the county’s own map, and say plainly that the condition of the concrete is a question that comes before the question of what to put on it.

Inside the house

The other failure: water you cannot see

Most houses here are built on a slab, and a slab on grade is in permanent contact with the ground. Water vapor moves from the ground up through the concrete toward the room. A coating is a comparatively airtight film laid across the top of that. Where the vapor drive is strong enough, pressure builds underneath and the film blisters or releases.

Whether a vapor retarder was laid under your slab was decided the day the house was poured and cannot be changed now. An older house here may have none. That does not rule out a coated floor. It rules out guessing.

There are three published tests and they do not measure the same thing:

  • ASTM F2170 reads the internal relative humidity from probes set in holes drilled to 40 per cent of the slab depth. It tells you what the slab will do over time, and it has been displacing the older method since 2002.
  • ASTM F1869 calculates a moisture vapor emission rate from the weight a dish of calcium chloride gains under a sealed dome over three days. It reflects the surface, not the interior, and since 2010 the standard has disallowed it on lightweight concrete.
  • ASTM D4263, the plastic sheet method, is the quick one — used, in the standard’s own words, “to indicate the presence of capillary moisture in concrete.” It shows surface conditions only.

You do not have to know any of that. You have to know it exists, that it produces a number, and that you can ask for the number in writing. We will run it and hand you the reading whether or not you hire us.

Commercial and industrial

The slab underneath

Almost every failure is a preparation failure

Concrete finishes with a weak, fines-rich film on its surface called laitance. It is a normal product of how a slab is poured and floated, not a defect. It is also not something a coating can bond through. A finish rolled over laitance is bonded to the laitance, and the day the laitance lets go, the floor looks like the coating failed.

The requirement is published, and not by us. Sika’s surface preparation guide puts it this way: concrete substrates “must be mechanically prepared to remove cement laitance, existing coatings, curing compounds, and achieve a profile that is clean, dry, and free from dirt, grease, oil.” Diamond grinders, in the same guide’s words, “are used to create a surface profile, remove high spots on a concrete surface and remove coatings.” Shot blasting it calls “the industry standard for surface preparation of concrete.”

What grinding leaves behind has a number. The International Concrete Repair Institute publishes the scale in Guideline 310.2R-2013, running from CSP 1 — essentially unchanged concrete with the loose debris removed — through CSP 9 and beyond at exposed aggregate. Physical replica chips exist so a prepared slab can be held against the number and accepted or rejected. Most coating manufacturers now state the profile their product needs on its own data sheet.

That is why the useful question is not do you grind. It is what profile did you grind to, and how did you check it. Ask us. Ask whoever else is quoting you.

Questions we get asked

Four of these do not have an answer yet, and they are the four everybody else answers with a number. Where that is the case it says so and says why.

Common questions

01What does a garage floor cost?

There is no price on this page, and there will not be one until it is a number that holds on the fiftieth job rather than the easiest one. A published price that gets broken is a deceptive practice, not a bad review.

What decides it is the slab, not the square footage: how much grinding it needs, what is already on it, what the cracks are doing and what the moisture reads. That is why we look before we quote, and why a price quoted over the phone from a room size is a guess.

02How long will it last?

Most pages selling this in Washington County say fifteen to twenty years. We have not been able to trace that figure to a manufacturer, a standard or a study, so we are not going to repeat it.

What we can tell you is what decides it, and it is not the brochure. It is whether the laitance came off, whether the slab was tested for moisture, whether the cracks were repaired or covered, and whether the product going down is rated for the sun it will get. Those are all checkable before the work starts.

03Is polyaspartic better than epoxy?

The honest answer is that “better” is the wrong frame, and that most of what is written about it online is written by people selling one of them.

Ultraviolet stability is a property of a specific product, and the manufacturer publishes it. Sherwin-Williams describes polyaspartic floor coating as part of the urethane family and offering “UV-resistant, color-stable protection”; its aliphatic polyaspartic products state UV stability by name on the data sheet. That is checkable, which means its absence is checkable too.

So the question to ask is not which chemistry. It is which product is going on my floor, and what does its data sheet say. Ask us, and ask anyone else quoting you.

04How long before I can park on it?

That is set by the product and by the temperature of the slab, and it is printed on the data sheet for the specific system going down. You will be told it before the work starts, in writing, alongside the date.

We are not going to print a number here. “One day” is the industry’s favorite claim and it is true of some systems, in some conditions, at some temperatures. A slab at 105°F and a slab at 45°F are not the same job.

05Can you go over my existing coating?

Only if we can establish what it is and that it is sound, and usually we cannot. The safe answer is that it comes off. More to the point, if a coating has already failed once, the first job is finding out why — because a new one over the same unaddressed cause fails the same way. That is its own job and we price it as one.

06Do I have to empty the garage?

Yes, completely, and it is the part people underestimate every time. The floor is ground wall to wall, so nothing can stay in it — including the things on shelves, because grinding dust reaches them. We will tell you exactly what has to be out and by when, and we would rather have that conversation a week early than on the morning.

07Will the cracks come back?

A crack that is not moving is repaired and stays repaired. A crack that is moving — because the slab is settling, heaving or built over something that is — will telegraph back through any coating, usually within a season. We look at what the cracks are doing before quoting anything over them, and if the answer is that the slab is moving, we will tell you that a coating is money spent twice rather than take the job.

08Does my HOA have to approve it?

Your documents are the only authority and we are not going to guess at them. As a general shape: the inside of a garage is almost never an association’s business, and an exterior surface visible from the street sometimes is. We will read yours before you commit. Our HOA pages cover what the recorded documents in the larger communities here actually say.

09Is it slippery?

It depends entirely on the finish, and that is a decision rather than an accident. A full flake broadcast has real texture and grips well wet. A smooth solid color or a metallic does not, which is why we will not put a metallic on a pool deck even if you ask for one. Outside, texture comes before color in the conversation, every time.

10Do you do commercial work?

Yes — retail and showroom floors, warehouses, shops and service bays. The technical job is the same; what changes is that the schedule belongs to you. Work gets sequenced in sections, overnight or across a closure you choose, and which of those applies changes the price openly rather than quietly. Commercial floor coating and warehouse floors have the detail.
