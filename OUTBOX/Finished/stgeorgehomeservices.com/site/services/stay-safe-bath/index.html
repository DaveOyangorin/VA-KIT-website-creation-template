What the work is

Doing a bathroom in pieces means paying for access four times and living with a room that is half changed for a year. Doing it as one project means one scope and one schedule.

What goes into it depends on the assessment, not on a package name. A typical scope covers the shower entry, blocking and bars where you actually reach, a seat, the toilet, lighting, and the floor surface.

Everything in it is a service on this site with its own page. Nothing is bundled to hide it.

What is included

  • An assessment first, with the scope written before anything is bought
  • The shower entry, however your floor requires it to be done
  • Blocking through the wet walls, and bars placed to how you move
  • A seat, a toilet at the right height, and lighting

What is not

  • Anything not written in the scope you approved
  • Painting and decorating beyond making good
  • Electrical, which is our licensed electrical partner's and quoted in
  • A price before the assessment. We are not going to quote a bathroom over the phone

It starts with the assessment, and the assessment stands alone

You can buy the assessment on its own and take the report to anyone. We would rather you did that than felt committed by having had someone in the house.

Our team includes a Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist. That certification belongs to a person, not to a company, and we describe it that way because it is the truth.

What the evidence says

The guide below covers what the research actually shows about home modification and falls, including where the finding is a high-certainty null. We would rather point at that than sell on fear.

What we can show you in writing

Every line traces to a document we read in full. The number after a sentence points to the research below.

  • The Access Board says it in its own words: the ADA does not apply to individually owned housing, single-family homes included. Nobody is going to inspect your bathroom, and nobody can require any of this. We use the published dimensions because they are the best-tested numbers anyone has.1
  • The turning space everyone quotes is a 60-inch circle, which does not exist in a 5-by-8 bathroom. The same standard publishes a T-shaped alternative inside a 60-inch square, and allows doors to swing into it. That is usually what a small bathroom here can actually be given.1
  • There is no required slip-resistance number anywhere, in a house or out of it. The federal standard says so itself, because no agreed test method exists to set one against. What we do instead is pick tile whose maker publishes a wet friction figure for interior wet floors, and tell you which tile and what the figure is.1
  • A federal health institute's own list puts bars near the toilet and on both the inside and the outside of the tub and shower. The outside one is the one that gets left off, and it is the one you hold while your feet are wet and the floor is not.1
