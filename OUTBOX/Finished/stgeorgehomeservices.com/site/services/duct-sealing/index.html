What the work is

Ducts in an unconditioned attic leak into the hottest space in the house. Every cubic foot that escapes is cooling you generated, paid for, and then threw away, and it also pulls hot attic air back in through the return side.

We find the leaks, seal the joints and boots with mastic rather than cloth tape, and repair or replace runs that are crushed, disconnected or too small to carry what the system is trying to push through them.

This is often the fix for the room that will not cool, and it costs a fraction of the bigger system that gets recommended instead.

How we find them: pressurise the duct system and look for where it will not hold, then confirm by hand at the joints and boots. The usual offenders are boots at the register, where the duct meets the ceiling, and the plenum connections at the equipment.

The other half of the job is balance. Sealing a leaky system changes the pressure everywhere in it, so registers that were roughly right can end up over- or under-served. We measure the rooms afterwards and adjust, because a sealed system that now overcools the front bedroom has moved the problem rather than fixed it.

What is included

  • The leaks found and shown to you before they are sealed
  • Mastic on joints, boots and plenums
  • Crushed or disconnected runs repaired or replaced
  • The registers checked for what they actually deliver afterwards
  • Room registers measured after sealing, and dampers adjusted

What is not

  • Cloth duct tape, which fails in an attic and is why some of these joints are open in the first place
  • Full duct replacement, which is a separate quote after we have looked

What we can show you in writing

Every line traces to a document we read in full. The number after a sentence points to the research below.

  • ENERGY STAR publishes that in a typical house about 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through the duct system is lost to leaks, holes and poorly connected ducts. That is EPA's national figure for a typical house. No Washington County number exists, so we do not pretend one does.1
  • The physics is not in dispute and the federal lab states it plainly: more airflow resistance lowers system airflow, which reduces heat exchanger efficiency and raises duct pressure — and higher duct pressure means more leakage out of the ducts you already have. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory monitored ten forced-air systems for a year to measure how far filters actually push that.1
  • Pacific Northwest National Laboratory publishes 0.4 to 0.5 inches of water column as the total pressure drop a residential system should stay under. It is a reading a gauge takes, which means your ducts can be measured rather than described.1
