Before You Backfill: A Foundation Checklist for Utah Builders

By Rocky Mountain Waterproofing · October 19, 2026

Request a Bid 801-282-2100

A foundation backfill checklist is the list of things to finish while the outside of a basement wall is still open: ties, rock pockets, joints, pipes, the coating, the drain and the grade. Once the dirt goes back, every one of them gets harder, slower and more expensive to fix, and most of them can only be reached again with an excavator or by tearing out finished drywall.

We’ve waterproofed Utah foundations since 1960, and nearly every leak we’re called back to fix started as something that could have been handled in an hour before backfill. Here is the list we’d want every builder to walk before the excavator comes back, with the Utah Residential Code sections (the 2021 IRC as adopted in Utah) that apply.

1. The wall can take the load

Backfill pushes sideways on a wall that was designed to be held at the top by the floor framing. The code is plain about it: backfill should not be placed against the wall until the wall has sufficient strength and has been anchored to the floor above, or braced well enough that the backfill can’t damage it (R404.1.7). The exception is a wall holding back less than 4 feet of unbalanced fill. Backfilling a tall basement wall before the floor deck is on is how walls crack, bow or get knocked by a machine, and a structural crack is one of the hardest leaks to fix.

2. Form ties are broken, patched and sealed

Every poured wall is full of form ties, the metal pieces that held the forms while the concrete cured. The forms come off; the ties stay in the wall. They’re normally snapped off and patched on the outside, but a tie that isn’t treated properly is a straight path for water. Break them, patch them and coat over them before anything else goes on the wall. More on tie leaks.

3. Honeycomb is grouted and patched

Honeycomb, or rock pockets, are spots where the cement paste didn’t fill in around the aggregate, usually because the wall wasn’t vibrated enough. It’s more common than most people think, and it’s easy to handle while the wall is open: the form crew grouts the pockets soon after stripping, then the waterproofer patches over them (we use roller-grade waterproofing, sometimes with a fabric strip embedded for reinforcement) before coating the whole wall. Missed, honeycomb acts like a pathway straight through the wall. More on honeycomb.

4. Cold joints are detailed

A cold joint forms wherever one pour of concrete ends and the next begins: footing to wall, or between pours on a long wall. It’s a formed crack, and in our area it’s the second most common leak source we find. There are three ways to seal it, and all of them depend on timing: a waterstop or joint sealant placed before the next pour (by the concrete contractor), a waterproofing joint detail on the outside before backfill (by the waterproofing contractor), or an injection repair from the inside before the basement is finished. The cheapest of the three is the one done before backfill. More on cold joints.

5. Pipe penetrations are sealed

Every house has at least one pipe through the foundation, usually the main water line. The opening is larger than most leak points, so when it leaks it soaks the surrounding finishes quickly. Seal around every pipe before backfill, and check again before the area around it is finished inside. More on pipe penetrations.

6. The coating matches the space

The code minimum is damp proofing from the top of the footing (or 6 inches below the top of the basement floor, whichever is higher) up to finished grade (R406.1). Where a high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known, the code requires waterproofing instead (R406.2).

The bigger question is what the space will become. Damp proofing is a thin asphalt coat, 10 to 20 mils, that slows vapor and cracks with the concrete. Waterproofing is rubber-modified, at least 40 mils and up to 80 or more, and stretches over cracks as they open. On our comparison chart damp proofing is rated for no water pressure at all, while our waterproofing options are rated from 3 feet up to 8 or 12 feet of water at a crack. A crawlspace and a finished custom basement shouldn’t get the same coating. Compare the options.

7. The footing drain is in and goes somewhere

Most basements need a drain at the footing (R405.1). The code exceptions are well-drained gravel and sand soils, or a lot covered by a geotechnical report that says no drain is needed. A traditional gravel drain has to extend at least 1 foot beyond the outside edge of the footing and 6 inches above its top, covered with an approved filter membrane, and the water has to discharge somewhere: by gravity or a pump to an approved drainage system.

We usually install strip drain instead of pipe and gravel. It’s an engineered system that ICC stands behind, set vertically at the base of the wall or the face of the footing, and it doesn’t need a gravel bed in most cases. Whatever the system, confirm the outlet before the trench is filled. More on foundation drains.

8. Drainage or protection board is on

A drainage board between the wall and the backfill does two jobs: it lets water fall straight down to the footing drain instead of building pressure against the wall, and it protects the coating from rocks in the fill. Drainage mat, Warm-N-Dri and Thermal Drain each add drainage, and the last two add insulation. Thermal Drain also adds strength to the waterproofing, as long as it goes on the same day as the coating. Compare the boards.

9. The grade falls away from the wall

Finally, the backfill itself. The code calls for the grade to fall at least 6 inches within the first 10 feet from the foundation (R401.3), and backfill settles, so plan for it. Downspouts should carry water at least 5 to 10 feet from the house, and sprinklers should face away from the wall. A perfect coating and drain can still be overwhelmed by a yard that sends every storm toward the basement.

Walk it before the excavator comes back

None of these items takes long while the wall is open. If you’d like us to look at a foundation before backfill, or bid the waterproofing and drain together, send us the plans.

Questions

01When can you backfill a new foundation wall?

Under the Utah Residential Code (IRC R404.1.7), not until the wall has enough strength and is anchored to the floor above or braced so the backfill can’t damage it. Walls holding back less than 4 feet of unbalanced fill don’t need bracing.

02Is damp proofing enough before backfill?

It meets the code minimum for most basements (R406.1). Where a high water table or other severe soil-water conditions are known, the code calls for waterproofing instead (R406.2). For a basement you plan to finish, waterproofing is the better match either way.

03Does every foundation need a footing drain?

Most do. R405.1 requires drains around concrete or masonry foundations that retain earth and enclose space below grade, except on well-drained gravel and sand soils, or where a geotechnical report covering the lot says one isn’t needed.

Source: Utah Residential Code 2021, Chapter 4 (Foundations), sections R401.3, R404.1.7, R405.1, R406.1 and R406.2. Always check the current code and your local building department’s amendments.
