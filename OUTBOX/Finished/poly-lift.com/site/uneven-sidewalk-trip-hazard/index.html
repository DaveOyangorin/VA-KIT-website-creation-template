When Is an Uneven Sidewalk a Trip Hazard? The Quarter-Inch Rule, Explained

From the Poly-Lift blog · October 20, 2026

Free Estimate (435) 999-9309

A sidewalk becomes a trip hazard long before it looks dramatic. The published reference point is small: under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, a change in level of more than a quarter of an inch can no longer be a plain vertical edge. Past half an inch, it needs a ramp. Here is what those numbers mean, why slabs in Cache Valley drift out of them, and what can be done.

We lift sunken sidewalks, driveways, steps and garage floors around Logan and across Utah. The question people ask most often isn’t how the foam works. It is “is this bad enough to fix?” This is the honest way to answer it.

The numbers: what the ADA Standards say

The U.S. Access Board publishes the 2010 ADA Standards. Section 303 covers “changes in level”, which is the technical name for a lip between two slabs:

  • Up to 1/4 inch may be vertical (303.2). A plain edge that small is allowed.
  • Between 1/4 and 1/2 inch must be beveled, with a slope no steeper than 1:2 (303.3). The standard’s own note: a quarter inch vertical plus a quarter inch beveled, never more than half an inch combined.
  • More than 1/2 inch must be ramped (303.4), which on a sidewalk in practice means the slabs need to be brought back into line.

The same standards ask that walking surfaces be stable, firm and slip resistant (302.1), with a running slope no steeper than 1:20 and a cross slope no steeper than 1:48 (403.3). A slab that has tipped toward a house or a gutter can fail the cross-slope number even with no lip at all.

Does that apply to your sidewalk?

Strictly, the ADA Standards apply to places the ADA covers: businesses open to the public, and state and local government facilities. A private home’s walkway generally isn’t covered. But the numbers are the clearest published answer to “how much is too much”, and they are a good yardstick for a front walk that a grandparent, a stroller or a delivery driver uses every day. For a business, an HOA or a property manager, they matter more directly.

Who has to repair the public sidewalk in front of a property varies from city to city. If the slab is in the park strip or along the street, check with your city before doing anything.

Why slabs here drift out of level

Concrete doesn’t usually sink because of the concrete. It sinks because of what is under it:

  • The base. If the base under a slab was never compacted properly, the slab settles as it cures and gets heavier, and that can happen quickly.
  • Freeze and thaw. When frost is in the ground, it expands and heaves slabs up. When it melts, they settle, often not back to where they started. That is how trip spots appear after a winter.
  • Water. Damaged water or sewer lines, and downspouts that pour water next to a slab, wash out the base material and leave voids.
  • Dry spells. Drought can shrink expansive clay soils under a slab, and wet conditions can make them swell again.

Once one slab drops, the joint next to it becomes the lip. Water then gets into that joint and under the slab, and the problem tends to grow.

How to measure a lip yourself

  1. Lay a straight board or a level across the joint, resting on the higher slab.
  2. Measure the gap down to the lower slab with a tape or ruler.
  3. Note anything over a quarter inch, and anything over half an inch especially.
  4. While you are there, check whether the slab tilts toward the house. Water running back toward a foundation is a second problem.

Take a photo with the tape in it. It makes an estimate faster and gives you a record of whether the slab is still moving.

The fixes, from smallest to largest

  • Grinding can take down a small lip on a slab that is otherwise sound and not moving. It doesn’t fix what is under it.
  • Lifting raises the sunken slab back into line by filling the void under it. Our method, Deep Foamjection, injects polyurethane foam through small holes. The foam is light compared with grout, the slab can be used almost straight away, and most jobs take less than a day. Cracked slabs can be lifted too.
  • Replacing is the answer when a slab is broken up beyond repair. It costs more and takes longer; lifting is usually around half the cost of replacement or less.

After a lift, sealing the surface and the joints helps keep water out of the base, which is what started the trouble in the first place.

When to call

If a lip is past a quarter inch, if it is getting bigger each spring, or if a slab is tipping toward your house, it is worth an estimate. We’ll look at the slab, tell you whether lifting is the right fix, and give you the number. Read more about how effective polyurethane foam is for lifting concrete.

Questions

01How high does a sidewalk lip have to be to count as a trip hazard?

There is no single legal number for every sidewalk, but the 2010 ADA Standards are the common reference: a change in level of up to 1/4 inch may be vertical, 1/4 to 1/2 inch must be beveled, and more than 1/2 inch must be ramped. A lip past a quarter inch is where most people start paying attention.

02Does the ADA apply to my home's sidewalk?

The ADA Standards apply to places the ADA covers, such as businesses open to the public and state and local government facilities. A private house is generally not covered, but the same numbers are a useful yardstick for what is safe to walk on.

03Can a cracked, sunken slab be lifted, or does it need replacing?

Cracked concrete can be leveled. Lifting usually costs around 50% or less than replacing, and most jobs are done in less than a day.

Source: U.S. Access Board, 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, sections 302.1, 303.2–303.4 and 403.3. Who repairs public sidewalks varies by city: check with yours.
