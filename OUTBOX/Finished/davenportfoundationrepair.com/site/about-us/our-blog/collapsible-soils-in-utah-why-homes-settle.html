Collapsible Soils in Utah: Why Some Homes Settle When the Ground Gets Wet

Some Utah soils hold a house up perfectly well, until water reaches them. Here is what collapsible soils are, where they occur, and what homeowners can do.

Davenport Foundation Repair · October 19, 2026 · 6 min read

Collapsible soils are dry, loosely packed soils that are strong in their natural state but settle when they get wet. The Utah Geological Survey calls the process hydrocompaction: water weakens the bonds between grains, the air spaces close up, and the ground drops. Under a house, that can mean a corner or one side of the foundation sinking, often after years of no trouble at all.

Strong when dry, weak when wet

Most people expect the ground under a house to be either good or bad. Collapsible soil is both. In its dry, natural state it has considerable strength and can carry a foundation without complaint. The grains are held together at their contact points, by capillary tension in the small amount of water present or by binders such as silt, clay or salt, according to the Utah Geological Survey.

When more water arrives, those bonds weaken. The grains slide into the open spaces between them and the soil takes up less volume. Nothing about the surface warns you beforehand: the change happens below the footing, out of sight, and often long after the house was built.

Where collapsible soils occur in Utah

The Utah Geological Survey associates collapsible soils with young alluvial fans, debris-flow deposits and loess, the fine, wind-blown silt. Alluvial fans form where streams leave the mountains and spread their sediment out, which is exactly where much of Utah has built: along the foothills of the Wasatch Front, in Utah County and in southwestern Utah.

Not every alluvial fan is collapsible, and not every lot on one has a problem. But it is one of the reasons two houses on the same street can behave very differently.

How much the ground can drop

The amount depends on how thick the susceptible layer is and how much water reaches it. In a Utah Geological Survey study of the St. George–Hurricane area, collapsible deposits could settle up to 10 percent of their thickness when first wetted. Even a fraction of that, under one corner of a house, is enough to crack walls and tilt floors.

The survey’s summary for homeowners is plain: collapsible soils lose volume when saturated, and the subsidence that follows can cause structural damage.

What wets the soil under a house

Rain and snowmelt play a part, but in many cases the water is something we add ourselves:

  • Irrigation against the foundation. Lawns, flower beds and drip lines watered right up to the wall, season after season.
  • Downspouts that dump at the wall. A whole roof’s worth of water concentrated in one spot. Downspout extensions carry it away from the house.
  • Grading that slopes toward the house. Water should run away from the foundation, not toward it.
  • Leaking water and sewer lines. A slow leak under or beside a house can wet soil for months before anyone notices.

That is why settlement on collapsible soil often starts at one corner or along one side: the side with the sprinklers, the downspout or the leak.

Signs your house may be settling

  • Stair-step cracks in block or brick, or cracks that are wider at one end.
  • Doors and windows that start to stick or won’t latch.
  • Floors that slope or feel uneven.
  • Gaps where walls meet ceilings or floors.
  • A chimney or porch pulling away from the house.
  • Sunken concrete next to the foundation, where the same wet soil has dropped under the slab.

Our page on foundation settlement shows what these look like in more detail.

What can be done

The first step is always to control the water: extend downspouts, pull irrigation back from the foundation, fix the grading and repair any leaking lines. That slows or stops further collapse.

If the foundation has already settled, the usual fix is to stop relying on the problem soil altogether. Steel push piers are driven through the weak layer to firmer soil below, and the weight of the house is transferred onto them. Where the ground has dropped under a driveway, walkway or patio, the slab can often be lifted back into place with concrete leveling.

What an inspection looks at

When settlement is the question, the inspection looks at the whole house, not just the worst crack. That means measuring floor levels across rooms, checking the pattern and width of cracks inside and out, looking at doors, windows and the chimney, and walking the outside to see where water goes: downspouts, sprinkler heads, grading and any wet or sunken areas next to the foundation. Together those clues show which part of the house is moving and why, which is what decides the repair.

Every house is different, and the right repair depends on what is actually happening below grade. A free inspection is the place to start.

Questions

01What are collapsible soils?

Soils that are strong when dry but settle when they get wet. The Utah Geological Survey describes them as losing air space within the soil (hydrocompaction) when wetted, which can make the ground beneath a foundation drop.

02Where are collapsible soils found in Utah?

The Utah Geological Survey associates them with young alluvial fans, debris-flow deposits and wind-blown silt (loess). Alluvial fans are common along Utah's mountain fronts, where many neighborhoods are built.

03How much can these soils settle?

In a Utah Geological Survey study of the St. George–Hurricane area, collapsible deposits could settle up to 10 percent of their thickness when first wetted. How much a given home settles depends on the deposit and how much water reaches it.

04What wets the soil under a house?

Usually water we add: lawn and garden irrigation against the foundation, downspouts that empty at the wall, poor grading, and leaking water or sewer lines.

05Can settlement from collapsible soil be repaired?

Often, yes. Piers driven through the problem soil to firmer layers can stabilize a settling foundation, and sunken slabs can be lifted. The first step is an inspection to confirm the cause.
