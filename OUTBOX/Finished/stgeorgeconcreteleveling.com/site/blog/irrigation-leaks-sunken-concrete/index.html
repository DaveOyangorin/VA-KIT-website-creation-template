Is Your Sprinkler System Sinking Your Concrete? Finding Irrigation Leaks Before They Wash Out a Slab

By St. George Concrete Leveling · October 20, 2026

Free Quote 435-466-5545
Water under the slab

In St. George, the most common reason a pool deck, walkway or driveway edge sinks isn’t the concrete. It is water underneath it, and very often that water comes from the irrigation system. A leaking valve, a broken head or a sprinkler that sprays the slab every night can wash out the base a slab sits on, and the slab settles into the gap. Here is how to find those leaks, and why it matters before anyone lifts a slab.

We see the pattern constantly: pool decks in Southern Utah often settle from washouts and irrigation leaks, and walks beside planted beds drop at the edge nearest the drip line. Lifting the slab fixes the symptom. Finding the water fixes the cause.

Why irrigation water ends up under concrete

A slab is only as stable as the soil and base under it. When water keeps reaching that layer, it carries fine material away and leaves voids. Here, red soils and desert moisture swings add to it: some soils pool water when it arrives faster than they can take it in, and the EPA notes that clay-rich soils and slopes are prone to exactly that when water is applied too quickly.

Irrigation is the most regular source of that water. The EPA’s WaterSense program says homes with clock-timer irrigation use about 50 percent more water outdoors than homes without, and a system that is programmed wrong, has a head pointed the wrong way or has a leak can waste even more. Much of it runs at night, so nobody sees it.

Five places to look

  1. Broken or tilted heads. Broken heads are hard to spot because most systems run overnight or early in the morning, and the EPA notes that running with broken or damaged heads can cause overflowing and flooding. Run each zone by hand in daylight and watch.
  2. Heads aimed at the hardscape. If a sprinkler is soaking the driveway, the sidewalk or the house wall, it is putting water straight onto the slab edge. The EPA’s advice is to turn those heads toward the landscape.
  3. Leaky joints. Leaks can occur where sprinklers connect to the piping. The EPA says leaking joints can also be a sign of too much water pressure, so check that a pressure regulator is installed properly.
  4. Valve boxes. Open the valve boxes and check that the valves close completely. If they don’t, the EPA warns the system can slowly seep water even when it’s turned off.
  5. Wet spots between cycles. Water pooling on the surface when the system hasn’t run recently can be a sign of an underground leak.

The meter test

For leaks you can’t see, the EPA’s Fix a Leak Week guide has a simple test: read your water meter, use no water at all for two hours, then read it again. If the meter has moved, you probably have a leak somewhere. Shut the irrigation supply and repeat the test to tell whether it is the sprinkler system or the house.

Watering habits that protect slabs too

  • Water at the right time. The EPA suggests avoiding the middle of the day, when the sun evaporates much of the water, and checking with your local water utility for how much and when to water.
  • Cycle and soak. On soils that pool, split each run into shorter intervals with breaks so water soaks in instead of running across the surface and under the nearest slab edge.
  • Keep drip lines out of slab edges. Emitters right against a walk or deck feed water directly into the base.
  • Seal the joints. Open joints let surface water straight into the base; sealing key joints is part of every lift we do.

Pool decks: the special case

Pool decks get water from every direction: splash-out, backwash, the auto-fill line, the irrigation around the pool fence and, sometimes, a leak in the pool plumbing itself. Before a deck is lifted, it is worth ruling out the pool. If the pool loses more water than evaporation explains, have the plumbing checked first, because no amount of lifting will hold a slab over a running leak.

When a deck is lifted, two things matter most. The pitch has to be put back so water drains away from the pool rather than toward the coping, and the material under the slab has to tolerate moisture. That is why we lift pool decks with water-resistant foam that won’t wash out, work carefully around the coping, landscaping and plumbing, and finish by sealing the joints where the deck meets the coping.

Signs the water has already done damage

  • A slab edge that has dropped beside a planter, lawn or pool.
  • A new lip at a joint, especially one getting bigger each season.
  • Water that now drains toward the house or the pool instead of away.
  • A hollow sound when you tap the slab, which can mean a void underneath.

If you see those, fix the water first, then get the slab looked at. Our void filling and soil stabilization work fills the gap the water left; pool deck, sidewalk and driveway leveling bring the slab back to grade. On the home page, the lip gauge shows when a lip becomes a trip hazard.

Questions

01Can a sprinkler leak really make concrete sink?

Concrete rests on the soil and base under it. Water that keeps running under a slab can wash out that base and leave a void, and the slab settles into it. That’s why pool decks and walks next to irrigated beds are common trouble spots here.

02How do I check for an underground irrigation leak?

The EPA suggests looking for water pooling on the surface between irrigation cycles. Its leak guide also suggests reading your water meter before and after two hours with no water in use; if it moves, you probably have a leak.

03Should I fix the leak before leveling the slab?

Yes. Lifting a slab without stopping the water that washed out its base leaves the cause in place. Fix the leak, then lift and fill the void, then seal the joints.

Sources: EPA WaterSense, “Sprinkler Spruce-Up”; EPA WaterSense, “Fix a Leak Week”; EPA WaterSense, “Watering Tips”.
