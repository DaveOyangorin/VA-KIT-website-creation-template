## **This is the build-first path to your first $1,000**

Instead of finding a buyer and then doing the work (that's the [Upwork Playbook](https://www.skool.com/rank-expand-academy/classroom/8c506815)), you build a lead-generating asset first, then find someone who'll pay for what it produces.

It'll take longer than Upwork to see the first dollar - first leads can come in as soon as a week, though most sites take a month or two to start ranking consistently - but you own the asset at the end

Done right, one microsite turns into a portfolio, and a portfolio turns into the kind of business that runs without you.

That's the long game. This module is about the short game: get one site live, ranked, and monetized.

## **What you'll learn**

- Pick a niche and location where a single new site can actually rank
- Build and publish a microsite in a few hours using the One-Click Blueprint
- Rank it for buyer-intent keywords using semantic branching
- Monetize it one of three ways:

- **Lease it** to a local business that wants the leads: simplest, fastest path to first $1k
- **Use it as proof** to land a local SEO retainer client: slightly higher ceiling, takes longer
- **Sell the leads directly**: needs volume, usually a second-site move once you've proven the concept

## **Best if**

- You're comfortable spending a small amount upfront — domain (~$15) and hosting (~$10/mo) per site
- You can wait 30 to 60 days to see ranking results before monetizing
- You like the idea of owning the asset rather than selling your time

## **Pick this over Upwork if**

- You've tried freelancing platforms before and hated the race-to-the-bottom dynamics
- You'd rather build once and earn repeatedly than pitch new clients every week
- You want a portfolio you can show off, sell, or scale

### 🆕 The Questions We Get Asked Most

This one exists because the same questions keep coming up, and most of them come from the same misunderstanding. Work through it once and niche selection stops being the thing that stalls you.

 

---

## **"The video says a map pack means too competitive. But every location I check has a map pack."**

This is the big one, and the video reads more strictly than we meant it.

Map pack size is not the metric. When we googled "emergency locksmith" and called it too competitive, that was not because a map pack appeared. It was because it is a bare primary keyword, the main service with nothing narrowing it down. "Emergency locksmith Tampa" is a local search with a location qualifier on it, and a local search is always going to be less competitive than the bare service term. Same map pack, completely different difficulty.

And to be straight with you: there is no exact science here. Anyone selling you a precise threshold is guessing.

Roy searched fifteen locations, the smallest at 50,000 people, across fifteen services each, and every single one had a map pack. That matches what we see. SERPs without map packs are close to extinct now. It is a leg up when you find one, but it is not the requirement, and waiting for one will keep you from ever launching. Almost all of our own sites compete against a map pack and pull leads just fine.

---

## **"Then what actually tells me a niche is winnable?"**

Three signals, all relative rather than absolute:

- Fewer map packs overall for the keyword
- Fewer ads on the results page
- Fewer reviews on the businesses that are ranking

Inside the Niche Validator, the Map Pack Score counts how many map packs show for that keyword. Fewer is a higher rating.

Mark Earle's version needs no tools at all, and he is running 50-plus sites on it. Put your keyword plus location in quote marks in the Google search box and look at how many competing pages come back. Sometimes you will find almost none. If the phrase makes sense to a human, build a page for it. In his words, do not overthink it and go for the low hanging fruit. When someone asked him about "concrete contractor modesto ca", that was 29 competitors, 90 searches a month, in a city of 219,000. That is a build.

---

## **"Where is the money actually hiding?"**

In the obscure long-tail service, not the headline one.

Our best results come from a secondary service sitting on a primary local EMD. So the domain targets emergency locksmith in Tampa, but the page that earns is "car lockout" or something similarly specific underneath it. The primary service gets you the domain and the shape of the site. The odd little service nobody optimizes for gets you the call.

A related point from Mark: do not chase zero search volume away. A keyword plus area with no reported volume is still fine. Volume tools do not see everything, and one job from a plumber pays for the site many times over.

---

## **"Should the domain be service plus city, or service plus state?"**

City-specific is the default and where most of our success sits.

Statewide can work. We have one state EMD outperforming a lot of our sites, but it is rare and the competition is much higher. If it lands, the lead flow is better.

For very large cities, go the other direction and drop a level, targeting neighborhoods or surrounding towns rather than the metro name.

If you are genuinely torn between two patterns, do not debate it. Build a few of each, run them through Google Keyword Planner for volume, and let the results tell you. That is the answer to almost every "which one" question in this model.

---

## **"One site per service area, or one site with lots of service area pages?"**

Both, and here is the shape.

- One primary service per site
- Plus a couple of extra service pages, which exist to bolster the primary keyword and make the site look like a real business rather than a one-page target
- Plus one to two dozen location pages on that same site

Then the next site sits about ten miles away and does the same thing. Neighboring sites are allowed to overlap on some of the same neighborhoods. That is not a problem, it is the design.

Mark's ratio, if you want a number to aim at: six to eight services across six to eight towns per site. He describes the portfolio logic plainly. Fifty sites at eighty pages each is four thousand tickets in the raffle.

---

## **"When do I build the Google Business Profile?"**

Usually you do not, at least not first.

We deliberately look for niches that do not need map pack rankings to produce calls. That is the whole point of the long-tail approach above. If you do build a GBP, the site gets more powerful, so it is a real upgrade and not a mistake. It is just not step one, and needing one is a sign the niche may be more competitive than you want.

---

## **"How do I stop second-guessing and actually pick one?"**

The honest answer is that the analysis has a floor, and past it you are just delaying.

Pick a service that is high ticket and low competition, ideally one where people have no brand loyalty. Nobody has a favorite company for a burst water line. They call whoever shows up first and seems trustworthy. That absence of allegiance is what you are buying.

Then build it. A site that is live and mediocre teaches you more in two weeks than another month of spreadsheets. If it is wrong, the next one costs you an afternoon.

### Publish Your Microsite

Your hosting is set up. Your site is in your downloads folder. Now you put one inside the other.

## The steps

1. **Unzip your site folder.** The Builder gave you a .zip. Extract it. You should see `index.html`, an `assets` folder, and your other `.html` files at the same level.
2. **Open your host's File Manager.** Log into your hosting dashboard and look for "File Manager" (sometimes called "Files"). Almost every host has this.
3. **Open your public folder.** The folder that holds your live site is usually called `public_html`, `www`, or `htdocs`. Open it. Anything in here shows up on your domain.
4. **Upload the contents of your unzipped folder.** Drag the files in, or use the Upload button. Important: upload the *contents* of the folder, not the folder itself. `index.html` needs to sit at the top of `public_html`, not one level deep.
5. **Keep the structure intact.** The `assets` folder comes along with everything inside it. If your CSS or images don't load after upload, this is almost always the reason.

> **Using FTP instead?** Some hosts only give you FTP credentials. Same idea, different tool. Grab FileZilla (free), connect with the credentials from your dashboard, navigate to `public_html`, drag your files in. The five steps above still apply.

## Check it worked

Visit your domain in your browser. You should see your home page. Click around: open a service page, an area page, your estimate page. Everything should load styled, with internal links working.

If something's off, it's almost always one of these:

- **Page loads but no styling.** The `assets` folder didn't come along, or it landed in the wrong place.
- **"Page not found".** Your files are one level too deep, or `index.html` isn't at the top of `public_html`.
- **Domain doesn't load at all.** Your domain isn't pointed at the hosting yet. Wait a few hours for DNS to settle, then check again.
